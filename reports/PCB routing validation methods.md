# Check the answer, not the router

The cheapest strong evidence about your router costs nothing and comes before any board is fabricated: judge every routed board with a checker the router did not write, and compare every net's length with a geometric floor. A routing result is hard to produce but easy to check, so the work is to build independent judges (KiCad's headless DRC with a fab rule file, a small polygon or raster checker of your own, Freerouting on the identical placement) and to prove each judge is live by seeding known violations. That ladder answers "is the layout correct and manufacturable" and "how efficient is the routing" in days of work and zero rupees. The physical board answers a narrower question than expected: for an LED, a 1 kHz RC filter, a divider, a DHT22 and an RS-485 node, calculated trace parasitics sit between one and a half and three and a half orders of magnitude below the tolerance band of the parts beside them — the RS-485 120 Ω termination is the tightest case at about 1.6 orders — so a working board says almost nothing about routing quality unless the order also carries a hand-routed reference, a deliberately bad control and a coupon strip. That whole physical experiment fits in roughly INR 750-1,200 of fabrication before any duty. The honest state of the router today is that completion, not wirelength, is the problem: published practice counts a board only when it is fully connected and DRC-clean, and "3 of 4 nets" supports no percentage claim at all. Two things are not worth your money on these boards: RF-style trace measurement (NanoVNA TDR, full-wave solvers) and trace-heating campaigns.

## Ten methods ranked by confidence gained per rupee and hour

The table orders the methods by how much they change what you can honestly claim, divided by what they cost. Effort figures are my estimates for one developer who already has the Board IR and the A* router; they are not published numbers. The sections that follow explain each rung and the evidence behind it.

| Rank | Method | Cost | What a pass proves | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Per-net length over a lower bound; two-BFS diagnosis of every failed net; net-order permutation run | Hours, free | How far each route is above its geometric floor; whether a failure is a modelling bug, an ordering problem or a search-space problem | That the placement is good; that the floor is reachable |
| 2 | KiCad headless DRC with a fab rule file, as a second judge of your tracks | Days (needs `.kicad_pcb` export), free | Width, clearance, ring, hole and edge limits hold according to an engine you did not write | That the rule file matches the fab today; anything the exporter mis-states consistently |
| 3 | Seeded violations with a margin sweep, run through every checker | Days, free | Each checker fires when it should and stays quiet when it should | Rule classes with no injector |
| 4 | Single-net exact oracles (BFS reachability, Dijkstra cost) and routable-by-construction boards | Days, free | A* finds a path whenever one exists and finds the cheapest one; a "could not route" on a constructed board is a false negative | Multi-net optimality |
| 5 | Freerouting on the identical placement and rules | Days, free | Whether the board is routable at all under those rules; a reference length and via count | That either router is optimal |
| 6 | Fab DFM upload plus a scripted Gerber lint | Minutes per board, free, manual | This factory's CAM accepts these files | Function, footprint correctness |
| 7 | Closed-form parasitics and a back-annotated ngspice run | Hours, free | A bound on how much traces move each graded quantity | Any individual parasitic to better than 10-20% |
| 8 | Fabricate generated, reference and bad-control boards with a coupon strip; continuity, four-wire resistance, functional timing | About INR 750-1,200 plus a multimeter and logic analyser; two to three weeks | Copper matches the netlist; trace length and width are what the Board IR says; generated routing is electrically equivalent to a reference for these circuits | EMC, fast-edge behaviour, other batches or fabs |
| 9 | Public benchmark boards (PCBWorld D3-A) | Weeks (needs an importer) | A clean-pass rate comparable with published baselines | Anything about your own templates' placement |
| 10 | Exact multi-net SAT model, mutation testing, a formal grid-to-millimetre lemma | Weeks | Optimality gap and grid-scoped unroutability proofs on tiny boards | Scaling is unmeasured |

Rank 8 sits low not because it is weak but because rungs 1 to 7 make it interpretable. A fabricated board that fails after skipping them cannot be attributed to the router, the exporter, the checker or the fab.

## Efficiency needs a floor and a reference, not a copper total

The metric set used for PCB routers in 2026 is clean pass (fully connected and zero DRC violations), routability, violation count, total wirelength, via count and wall-clock time, with **wirelength and vias compared only among results that are complete and clean**. PCBWorld, an open benchmark built on the KiCad engine, has KiCad itself compute violations, connectivity, length and vias, and treats clean pass as the primary measure ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915v4)). Freerouting publishes a second, independent idea: a score for how far a finished routing sits above its geometric floor, using the Manhattan minimum spanning tree as the minimum length, minimum layer changes as the via floor and minimum corners as the bend floor. Every percent above the length floor costs linearly, and 100% over costs the full 1000 points ([Freerouting scoring](https://github.com/freerouting/freerouting/blob/master/docs/scoring.md)).

That gives you the missing number. A figure such as "54.2 mm of copper" cannot be judged without the sum of per-net floors for the same nets. Half-perimeter wirelength is exactly the optimal rectilinear Steiner length for two- and three-pin nets and a lower bound above that ([Kahng et al.](https://ifte.de/books/eda/chap4.pdf)), and nearly every net on a hobby board has two or three pins, so HPWL computed in a dozen lines of Python is the correct floor here. Keep the two families straight: **HPWL and the rectilinear Steiner minimal tree are genuine lower bounds; a minimum spanning tree and an iterated 1-Steiner tree are not** — adding Steiner points can only shorten a tree, so MST sits *above* the optimum as an estimate (within 3/2 of it), not below it as a floor. For multi-pin nets, use RSMT as the floor, or bound it from below by two-thirds of the rectilinear spanning tree ([Hwang's bound](https://www.csie.ntu.edu.tw/~kmchao/tree21spr/Steiner.pdf)). FLUTE computes RSMT quickly and is exact up to nine pins, which covers every net here; the OpenROAD `flute3` fork is BSD-3-Clause ([flute3](https://github.com/The-OpenROAD-Project-Attic/flute3)), while GeoSteiner is an exact solver free for non-commercial use only, under CC BY-NC 4.0 ([GeoSteiner licence](http://geosteiner.com/LICENSE)). One trap: match the distance metric to the router's moves. If the router lays 45-degree segments, a Manhattan floor can be beaten and the ratio becomes meaningless, so use octile distance there. A tighter and still honest floor is to route each net alone on the otherwise empty board with the same A*; the gap between that sum and the full routing isolates what net-to-net interference costs. No source publishes a "good" ratio for PCBs. My judgement, anchored on the spread below, is that uncongested two-pin nets should land within about 1.0 to 1.3 of the floor and a net at 2x deserves a look.

The reference comparison is the other half. On 99 small real boards (2 to 13 nets), Freerouting 2.1.0 achieved an 80% clean pass with 91% routability, a Rust A* router achieved 74%, and mean wirelength was 170.2 mm for the original human routings, 156.2 mm for Freerouting and 142.8 mm for the A* router, which used three times the vias ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915v4)). Three lessons follow. Competent routers land within roughly 15% of one another on small boards, so length tuning has little headroom. Length and vias must be read together. And **even the mature open-source router fails one small real board in five**, with 91% net-level routability coexisting with 80% clean pass, which is why the honest unit is boards, not nets. Placement also dominates: one study moved routed wirelength by up to 25% and vias by up to 50% through placement changes alone under the same router ([NS-Place](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.14259)), so a comparison is only valid on the identical placement, and a poor number may be a placement fault.

Running Freerouting on your placement is free. It is GPL-3.0, reads Specctra DSN, writes SES, and runs headless with `--gui.enabled=false -de board.dsn -do board.ses`, with `-mp` for pass count and `-drc` for a violation report in KiCad's JSON format ([Freerouting CLI](https://github.com/freerouting/freerouting/blob/master/docs/command_line_arguments.md)). The repository already has a `to_dsn()` bridge, so the missing piece is an SES reader; parsing the SES for segment lengths and via counts is enough to get a reference without any import step. Two cautions. Rules must be identical on both sides, because a reference routed at 0.2 mm clearance says nothing about a router held to 0.3 mm. And "neither router finished" proves nothing, since both are heuristics. The informative outcomes are "Freerouting routed it and A* did not", which is a false "unroutable" from your router, and "Freerouting's checker flags a board your kernel passed".

Before any of that, two free experiments explain a failure such as the 6 of 14 connections recorded for the room-monitor board. The router's own docstring says the result is order-dependent, and the literature agrees that sequential routing success depends on order ([UPenn ESE 535](https://www.seas.upenn.edu/~ese5350/spring2011/lectures/day12_6up.pdf)). So route each board under 30 to 100 random net orders and report best, median and worst completion. A board that ranges from 6 to 14 across orders has an ordering and rip-up problem; a board stuck at 6 under every order has a search-space problem such as pads off the grid or a halo sealing a pad. Then, for each failed connection, run plain breadth-first search on the board with only pads as obstacles and again with the committed routes. Unreachable in the first case is a modelling bug, not a routing problem at all. These are my proposed experiments, not published protocols, but they separate causes that need different fixes.

On sample size, be strict with yourself. With zero failures in n trials, the 95% upper bound on the failure rate is about 3/n ([rule of three](https://www.johndcook.com/blog/2023/08/30/first-time-seeing-a-rare-event/)), so "routes at least 90% of boards" needs about 30 boards with no failures, and nets on one board are correlated and do not count as separate samples. Five templates re-routed with jittered placement are still about five designs, although jitter is a valid robustness test. The closest public corpus is PCBWorld's D3-A set of 100 small real boards in `.kicad_pcb` form, with a synthetic two-layer set on which both baseline routers score 100% and which therefore works as a smoke test ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915v4)). Using it needs a `.kicad_pcb` importer, and the licence of those boards was not established in the research, so check before redistributing.

## Zero DRC errors means little until someone else counts

"0 DRC errors" from your own kernel is the weakest number you report, because the router's obstacle map and the kernel's checks encode one author's understanding of clearance. The research pattern that fixes this is the certifying algorithm: the program emits an output plus a witness, and a separate, simple checker validates it ([McConnell et al.](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/certifying-algorithms/)). For routing, the tracks are the witness. "This board is routed" needs no argument about A*; it needs a trustworthy checker, and the entire trust burden falls on a few hundred lines. Today `router.py` imports `drc` and `score` from the same kernel that judges it, so the check is not yet independent.

The practical independent judge is KiCad. `kicad-cli pcb drc` takes a `.kicad_pcb`, writes a JSON report and, with `--exit-code-violations`, returns 0 when clean and 5 when not ([KiCad CLI](https://docs.kicad.org/9.0/en/cli/cli.html)). Fab limits go in a `.kicad_dru` custom-rules file, and an MIT-licensed community set covers JLCPCB and PCBWay two-layer one-ounce boards ([kicad-druid](https://github.com/Cimos/kicad-druid)). Three details decide whether this check is worth anything. KiCad's defaults are not a fab's limits, so the exporter must emit the rule file and project file alongside the board. KiCad can silently drop a custom rule, which is why that project compiles a sentinel rule in CI to prove the file loaded ([kicad-druid](https://github.com/Cimos/kicad-druid)). And the format documentation describes KiCad 6-era syntax ([KiCad file format](https://dev-docs.kicad.org/en/file-formats/sexpr-pcb/index.html)), so the safest template is a tiny board saved by the KiCad version you install. This was run, not just read: KiCad 10.0.1 is installed at `C:\Program Files\KiCad\10.0`, and on a bundled demo board `kicad-cli pcb drc --format json --exit-code-violations` reported 28 violations and exited **5**, while the same board under `--severity-exclusions` (no excluded violations) reported none and exited **0**. One trap worth knowing: **without** `--exit-code-violations` the same 28-violation run exits **0**, so the flag is the whole gate and a CI step that omits it can never fail. A malformed board exits **3** (`Failed to load board`). The JSON report's fields are `violations`, `unconnected_items`, `schematic_parity`, `ignored_checks`, `included_severities`, `coordinate_units` (default `mm`), `kicad_version`, `date`, `source` and `$schema`; each violation carries `type`, `severity`, `description` and an `items` array with `pos` and `uuid`.

A second judge that needs no export is a checker written against the Board IR alone: buffer tracks into polygons with Shapely, union-find the shapes that touch, and require that every connected group holds pads of exactly one net and every net's pads sit in exactly one group; or rasterise each layer at a pitch far finer than the 0.25 mm routing grid and label connected regions. Different methods are worth more than a second copy of the same method, because they fail on different inputs. This is my design, built from the certifying-algorithm idea, not a published PCB practice; no source was found for a router that ships an independently authored checker.

Then prove the judges are live. KiCad's own regression suite keeps two sets of boards, ones that must yield zero errors and ones that must yield an exact violation count, each named after the bug that produced it ([KiCad DRC regressions](https://docs.kicad.org/doxygen/test__drc__regressions_8cpp_source.html)). Copy that: from a clean board apply one mutation (move a track to just under clearance, narrow it to just under minimum, shrink a via ring, delete a segment, bridge two nets, push copper past the edge) and expect exactly one finding of the matching class, plus the just-legal twin that must produce none. Sweep the margin around the threshold so the boundary behaviour becomes a tested decision. A run in which the oracle reports nothing on a seeded board is a harness failure, not a pass. Reading the kernel turned up one candidate defect this would settle: the track-to-track pre-filter pads bounding boxes by track width while the violation threshold uses clearance plus half-widths, so two narrow tracks under a larger clearance could be skipped. That is a reading, not a confirmed bug; write the test before believing it.

For the router itself, where there is no ground truth, three techniques manufacture some. Dijkstra on the same grid with the same costs is an exact oracle for a single net, and breadth-first search is an exact oracle for reachability, so `astar` disagreeing with either is an unambiguous bug. Boards generated from a known routing (draw non-crossing paths, put pads at the ends, delete the paths) are routable by construction, so any failure on them is a false negative. And metamorphic testing, the established answer to the oracle problem ([Segura et al.](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/110335/1/segura16-tse.pdf)), checks relations between runs: removing a net, enlarging the outline or reducing clearance can never make a routable board unroutable, because the old solution is still valid. It has published fault-detection results on grid path planners ([Zhang et al.](https://research.buaa.edu.cn/en/publications/testing-graph-searching-based-path-planning-algorithms-by-metamor/)) but no publication applying it to PCB routing was found, so the relation list is derived, not borrowed. Hypothesis supplies generation, shrinking to a minimal failing board, and stateful testing of edit sequences ([Hypothesis](https://hypothesis.readthedocs.io/en/latest/stateful.html); MPL-2.0). The oracle claim above is the literature's own: exact solvers are used to decide "unroutable" versus heuristic failure, and the ISPD 2000 exact-switchbox work states a problem is routable if and only if the exact formulation yields a solution ([exact switchbox](https://cecs.uci.edu/~papers/compendium94-03/papers/2000/ispd00/pdffiles/2_4.pdf)) — with the caveat that BFS and Dijkstra are single-net oracles, so every net may have a path and the board still be jointly unroutable through congestion.

The strongest of these is differential testing, and it already has a directly relevant precedent in this exact toolchain: **Cir-Fuzzer** applied differential testing across EDA schematic and simulation tool chains including KiCad and ngspice, generating functionally equivalent circuit variants and finding 12 real defects ([Cir-Fuzzer](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3803420)); the technique itself is McKeeman's, named in 1998 ([McKeeman](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/comp/150FP/archive/bill-mckeeman/DifferentailTesting.pdf)). Metamorphic testing has EDA applications beyond path planners, in Verilog simulators and synthesis ([VeriEQ](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3798235)) and in shader compilers ([FSHADER](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1109/ICSE48619.2023.00201)), but work on FPGA place-and-route testing reports that equivalence checking *misses* real placement and routing decision bugs ([FPGA P&R testing](https://www.doc.ic.ac.uk/~afd/papers/2026/FPGA.pdf)) — which is the same warning as above, that a checker agreeing with you is not the same as the router being right.

Two methodology points are worth adopting wholesale because they are the field's standard. **Fix the placement so only the router is under test** — VPR/VTR documents a routing-only mode driven by a supplied `.place` file ([VTR](https://docs.verilogtorouting.org/)), and SPEC CPU 2026 formalises it by feeding a routing workload a known-good placement precisely to separate placement quality from routing ([SPEC CPU 2026](https://www.spec.org/cpu2026/)). And **use a benchmark corpus rather than random boards**; the ISPD contests, the VTR circuit sets and PCBWorld itself are the norm, in part because benchmark construction papers found earlier random data poorly reflected real routability and had to re-inject realistic design rules ([ISPD 2015](https://ispd.cc/contests/15/web/benchmarks/ispd_2015_contest_benchmark_description.pdf)). If mutation testing is wanted, both Python tools are free and current: `mutmut` (BSD-3-Clause) and `cosmic-ray` (MIT), each releasing through 2026 ([mutmut](https://pypi.org/project/mutmut/), [cosmic-ray](https://pypi.org/project/cosmic-ray/)).

Manufacturability is a separate question with a free answer. Published minimums for two-layer one-ounce boards cluster at 0.10 to 0.15 mm track and space: JLCPCB lists 0.10/0.10 mm (4/4 mil) for 1–2 layers, minimum drill 0.15 mm, and an annular ring of at least 0.20 mm on plated holes ([JLCPCB capabilities](https://jlcpcb.com/capabilities/pcb-manufacturing)), PCBWay the same 0.10/0.10 mm with a 0.15 mm minimum annular ring and 0.15 mm drill ([PCBWay capabilities](https://www.pcbway.com/capabilities.html)), and OSH Park 6 mil with a 15 mil edge keepout ([OSH Park](https://docs.oshpark.com/services/two-layer/)). A JLCPCB page quoting 5/5 mil would be the conservative figure, not the current minimum, so a rule set built on 5/5 is safe rather than tight. A profile of 0.20 mm track and space, 0.30 mm via drill with a 0.70 mm pad, and 0.40 mm copper-to-edge clears every fab surveyed, and through-hole and 0805 boards lose nothing at those values; that profile is my synthesis of the fabs' tables. Fab DFM tools are the only checks that see the true fabrication files: JLCPCB's browser tool and NextPCB's HQDFM are free ([JLCPCB DFM](https://dfm.jlcpcb.com), [HQDFM](https://www.nextpcb.com/dfm-tool)). They need a manual upload, so they are a gate before ordering and not a CI step. The task they perform — extract a netlist from the copper and compare it with the intended one — is a named CAM step, and one free hosted tool does it: Sierra Circuits' Better DFM ingests an IPC-356 netlist alongside the Gerbers and reports the shorts and opens by net ([Better DFM](https://www.protoexpress.com/tools/pcb-dfm-tool/)), where IPC-D-356B (*Bare Substrate Electrical Test Data Format*) is the netlist/test-point format in question ([IPC-D-356B](https://www.electronics.org/TOC/IPC-D-356B.pdf)). No *open-source* implementation of that comparison was found.

Two blind spots survive every tool here. None checks that a footprint matches the real part, which is the class of bug this project already hit with SOT-23 pin order. And schematic parity in KiCad would compare two files compiled from the same CircuitIR, so a shared upstream error passes both.

## Traces do not move these circuits, so prove a bound instead

For the reference trace of 0.25 mm by 50 mm in one-ounce copper, sheet resistance of about 0.48 milliohm per square ([DigiKey forum](https://forum.digikey.com/t/resistance-values-of-pcbs/2082)) gives about 96 milliohm; the remaining figures in this paragraph are the research notes' own arithmetic from standard formulas, not measured values. Against a 1,590 ohm resistor and 100 nF capacitor, that resistance and about 2 pF of trace capacitance shift a 1 kHz cutoff by thousandths of a percent, while the resistor is a 1% part and the capacitor 10%. The LED loses about 1 mV. The DHT22 line gains 2 pF against tens to hundreds of picofarads of cable. For RS-485, Texas Instruments' guideline is that a stub stay under one-tenth of the driver's output rise time ([TI SLLA272D](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/slla272d/slla272d.pdf)) — a companion application note gives the more permissive rule of a stub under one-third of the transition time ([TI SNLA042A](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/snla042a/snla042a.pdf)), so treat one-tenth as the conservative bound and one-third as the outer limit. A 50 mm board trace is several times inside either even for an assumed fast 15 ns edge, and the cable, termination and bias decide the waveform. The driver edge time was assumed, not read from the MAX485 datasheet.

The honest product of a parasitic analysis here is therefore a bound, not a finding: a back-annotated simulation of these five templates will report "no change" every time. The cheap implementation is deterministic and fits the project's compiler rule: from the Board IR compute each branch's resistance by counting squares, add one lumped series R and L with half the capacitance at each end, emit a second netlist beside the ideal one, run both through the existing grader, and report the delta per claim. A statement such as "trace resistance at most 0.15 ohm at 85 degrees with 20% thin copper, cutoff shift at most 0.01%" survives every uncertainty. Extracted values are simulated figures with a declared scope, not bench evidence.

Know where the formulas stop. Microstrip impedance equations agree with numerical solutions closely near 50 Ω ([Altium](https://resources.altium.com/p/clearing-up-trace-impedance-calculators-and-formulas)), but only with a continuous plane under the trace — and the widely quoted IPC-2141A equations should not be treated as accurate in general: the standard's own 2018 errata **deleted** its printed ±2% / ±1.5% / ±1% accuracy claims, and published comparisons measure errors up to tens of percent for wide lines ([IPC-2141A](https://www.electronics.org/TOC/IPC-2141A.pdf), [formula comparison](https://f4inx.github.io/posts/microstrip-formulas-comparison.html)). On a two-layer board without a pour there is no microstrip, and inductance is set by the loop the return current takes. That points at the effects that are real on these boards and absent from the schematic simulation: the decoupling-capacitor loop, where a board without planes requires minimising the capacitor-to-device loop explicitly ([LearnEMC](https://learnemc.com/decoupling-for-boards-without-power-planes)), and the crystal, which Microchip wants within about 12 mm of the pins with load capacitors beside it ([Microchip](https://onlinedocs.microchip.com/oxy/GUID-E48B4A26-F201-4F0A-BCBA-16461582816A-en-US-4/GUID-AA04B64F-3C38-4E1C-8B51-49DE3CFE2419.html)). These are better enforced as geometric layout rules computed from the Board IR (loop area per decoupling path, distance from a fast net to a high-impedance node) than simulated. A bottom-layer ground pour turns most of them into non-issues, and recommending it is cheaper than modelling its absence.

If you want one field solver, FastHenry computes inductance and AC resistance of arbitrary conductor geometry without assuming a plane, and its companion FastCap/FasterCap computes capacitance the same way; both are free and ship in one Windows bundle, with FastHenry2 at version 5.2.0 ([FastHenry2](https://www.fastfieldsolvers.com/fasthenry2.htm), [FasterCap](https://www.fastfieldsolvers.com/fastercap.htm)). Note the licence is **not** MIT: the sources are per-branch, largely LGPL, so check before redistributing ([FastFieldSolvers downloads](https://www.fastfieldsolvers.com/dwnld02.htm)). Track segments map directly onto FastHenry's input. openEMS is a free full-wave solver ([openEMS](https://www.openems.de/)) that answers questions which, by the notes' estimate, begin near 100 MHz; it is overkill here. No published error figures comparing these open tools with measurement on FR-4 were found, so validate any solver setup against structures with known analytic answers before trusting it. IR-drop tools for KiCad exist ([padne](https://github.com/atx/padne)) but none documents validation against measurement, and for a router that produces tracks and no pours, a resistor network solved by an ngspice operating point does the same job.

## A thousand rupees of boards, if the order includes controls

Five two-layer 100 by 100 mm boards cost about USD 2 at JLCPCB or PCBWay, with economy shipping to India at USD 3 to 8 and 7 to 14 days; Indian fabs quote roughly INR 400 to 800 with 3 to 7 day lead times ([Zbotic order guide](https://zbotic.in/how-to-order-pcb-from-jlcpcb-and-pcbway-india-cost-guide/), [Zbotic Indian fabs](https://zbotic.in/indian-pcb-manufacturers-compared-2026-price-quality-lead-time/)). Treat these figures with care: they come from an Indian PCB vendor that competes with the fabs it compares, the listings are undated, and its duty figures are internally inconsistent. JLCPCB states that duties are the customer's responsibility and that it cannot predict them ([JLCPCB customs](https://jlcpcb.com/help/article/Customs,-Duties-and-Taxes)). No official Indian customs source was reached. Home etching is the wrong tool for this purpose: experienced users report 8 to 10 mil at best ([EEVblog](https://eevblog.com/forum/projects/home-made-pcb_s)), and without plated vias a failure could not be attributed to the router.

The order arrives partly tested. JLCPCB's flying-probe test for opens and shorts is free and covers the whole order under 50 pieces ([JLCPCB FAQ](https://jlcpcb.com/quote/pcbOrderFaq/Flying%20probe%20test)). That proves the copper matches the Gerbers. It cannot prove the Gerbers match the schematic, because a pin wired to the wrong net is wrong in the Gerbers too. So the first bench step is yours: on the bare board, before soldering, beep every pad pair of every net and check resistance between every pair of nets, against a table generated from the Board IR. An Arduino can automate it by driving one pin low and reading the rest with pull-ups; that procedure is a standard technique described from general practice, with no published design located. It detects opens and hard shorts and says nothing about trace quality.

Trace quality needs a four-wire measurement: force a known current, read the millivolt drop on separate sense points, and the lead resistance drops out ([Siborg](https://siborg.com/?p=186)). A hobby meter specified at ±(0.5% + 2 digits) on DC volts ([UT33D+](https://toolboom.com/en/digital-multimeter-uni-t-ut33dplus)) resolves a 100 milliohm trace to a few percent by the notes' arithmetic. The limit is not the meter. Finished copper thickness varies with foil tolerance, processing loss and plating ([NCAB](https://www.ncabgroup.com/faq/how-much-finished-copper-can-be-expected/)), and etched width is narrower than drawn by an amount no fab page stated. A measurement 20 to 30% from a nominal-thickness calculation is therefore not evidence of a routing fault. What the method does prove well is ratios on one board, where thickness is common: a routed net against a straight reference of the same width should match the ratio of their squares, which tests whether the router's length and width are what the Board IR says.

That is the purpose of a coupon strip along one edge of the panel, at no extra cost: straight traces at several widths (resistance against inverse width gives this batch's sheet resistance and etch loss), a via chain (per-via resistance, and an open chain exposes a ring or drill problem), a spacing comb at the router's minimum clearance, and two or three nets copied exactly from the generated layout beside straight references. The idea comes from IPC's daisy-chain coupons ([Sierra Circuits](https://www.protoexpress.com/kb/what-are-pcb-test-coupons/)); the specific strip is a proposal. Whether cheap fabs charge for structures that look like several designs on one order was not checked.

Function is where honesty matters most. Almost any connected routing works at these speeds, so order three variants and a control together: the generated routing, the same placement hand-routed or routed by Freerouting, a breadboard build with the same measured parts, and a deliberately bad board with a long minimum-width power trace, a distant bypass capacitor and split RS-485 lines. Write pass thresholds down before measuring. If the generated, reference and bad boards all give the same numbers, the correct conclusion is that these measurements cannot distinguish routing quality on this circuit. No published hobby-scale experiment of this shape was found; it is a proposal. The instruments that earn their place are a clone logic analyser, whose 24 MHz sampling covers DHT22 pulse widths and UART framing ([SparkFun](https://sparkfun.com/products/14789)) but sees only threshold crossings, never analog levels or the differential bus, and the Uno itself for DC voltages and for timing an RC charge, which the repository's `scripts/bench/rc_timer/` sketch already does. Pocket oscilloscopes at INR 3,395 to 6,690 advertise 200 to 500 kHz bandwidth ([Robu](https://stgaws.robu.in/product/fnirsi-dso152-oscilloscope-2-8-tft-handheld-digital-oscilloscope-2-5ms-s-high-sampling-rate-200khz-bandwidth)) and are optional. A NanoVNA covers 10 kHz to 1.5 GHz ([nanovna.com](https://nanovna.com/)), which starts above your 1 kHz filter, and trace impedance on a 1.6 mm two-layer board is far from 50 ohms, the condition under which TDR accuracy degrades ([IPC-TM-650](https://www.electronics.org/sites/default/files/test_methods_docs/2-5-5-7a.pdf)). Skip it. Skip trace heating too: the IPC-2221 formula ([Altium](https://resources.altium.com/p/ipc-2221-calculator-pcb-trace-current-and-heating)) puts a 0.25 mm one-ounce trace near 0.9 A for a 10 degree rise by the notes' arithmetic, against tens of milliamps on these boards, so the test cannot fail and tells nothing about the router.

For a real oscilloscope and a current-limited supply, a university electronics lab is the realistic free route; India's I-STEM portal is open to any citizen but its booking flow includes payment arranged with the operator ([I-STEM](https://www.istem.gov.in/support/about-us)). One afternoon with a two-channel scope and function generator would also close the project's outstanding RC filter accuracy gate. Five boards from one panel support "repeatable within one batch from one fab" and nothing wider.

## Conclusion

The framing that changes the work is that the router does not need to be trusted, only its output, and the output is checkable by things you did not write. That moves the durable investment away from the A* code, which the project already plans to replace with Freerouting, and into the checker, the seeded-fault suite, the corpus and the export path. All of them validate any router backend and survive the swap. It also changes the vocabulary of every report the engine produces: three verdicts instead of two, namely checked clean, proved within a stated scope (this grid, these rules, this epsilon), and not routed by this heuristic, which is not the same as unroutable.

The second shift is about what a physical board is for. On circuits this slow, fabrication does not test the routing's electrical quality; it tests the export chain, the footprints and the netlist, the places where this project has already found real bugs. The electrical question is better answered by a bound computed from the Board IR, and it only becomes a live question when a faster part enters the catalogue. The ESP32, with faster edges and current bursts of hundreds of milliamps, is the first place where a wider power trace and a tight decoupling loop stop being hygiene and start being measurable; that is the board on which to spend the second fabrication order.
