# Physics layout validation: what the market does and what Circuit OS can build

Physics layout validation exists. It is sold today, and much of it can be run with free tools. The earlier reports were right on one narrow point and wrong in how they said it. The narrow point holds: **no tool, free or commercial, proves that a board works**. Every vendor's physics result is a simulation on a model, or a rule check against a threshold, and no vendor claims more. But the reports had no word for "physics simulation". Their trust ladder ran from *bounded* to *evidence* to *unknown*, and they defined *unknown* as "no software check exists" (`Is the board correct`, about line 89). So "not provable here" turned into "not possible". Some statements were also plainly wrong or out of date. Siemens HyperLynx is not "the one stack" with electrical sign-off: Cadence, Ansys, Keysight and Altium all sell it. Board-level thermal, AC power-delivery impedance and placement scoring do exist as software. The `kicad-parasitics` project the plan "could not find" is on GitHub. The owner's market page overstates in the other direction. Quilter's "best-in-class physics validation" is eight rule checks on geometry and analytic estimates, a commercial 2D impedance solver, and a beta DC IR-drop tool. Quilter's own documentation tells users to keep running their own signal simulation. Everything Quilter and Flux compute today has a free or self-buildable equivalent. Circuit OS has none of it in code yet. That gap is unbuilt work, not impossibility, and most of it is days to weeks of work on the Board IR that already exists.

This report replaces the reading of the three earlier reports wherever they say "unknown", "cannot reach" or "no tool". Their proof results stand.

## How this was researched, and how far to trust it

Eight researchers worked in parallel on 2026-10-16: Quilter; Altium; the large sign-off vendors (Siemens, Cadence, Ansys, Keysight, Zuken); AI-PCB competitors; free physics tools; fab DFM and voltage/current checks; an audit of our own reports; and an audit of our own code. The Quilter researcher downloaded and read all 74 pages of Quilter's documentation (`docs.quilter.ai/llms.txt`), its changelog, its pricing page and four Project Speedrun posts. The code researcher ran the PCB tests (115 passed, 1 skipped) and ran `verify.py` on the DHT22 board.

Each load-bearing claim was then sent to two independent checkers: one re-opened the primary source, the other judged what kind of guarantee the result carries. The first pass hit its spend limit after the Altium, Siemens, Cadence, Ansys, Keysight and our-code claims. A second pass covered the rest: the Quilter, AI-PCB competitor, DFM, voltage-aware-checking and open-tool claims, one source checker per claim, run in batches. A technical reviewer then read the whole report against its sources. Of the 31 second-pass claims, 14 were confirmed as written and 17 were only partly supported, and this version corrects their wording. No claim was found to be wholly false. Where a checker could not open a page, or a figure was found only in a search snippet, the text says so. Labels used below:

- **checked**: confirmed by an independent re-read of the primary source
- **read**: the researcher opened the primary page; not independently re-checked
- **summary**: taken from a search snippet only
- **inference**: reasoning, ours or the researchers'

## The core confusion: four different things called "validation"

| What it is | What a pass means | Who has it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Formal proof** | True for every value in a stated model | Circuit OS (z3 over tolerance boxes, schematic level). No PCB vendor claims it | "LED current < 40 mA for all R, V_f, V_OH in tolerance" |
| **Physics simulation** | A numerical solver predicts this behaviour for this model. Accuracy rests on the model and on correlation to measurement | HyperLynx, Sigrity, SIwave, Keysight PIPro; Altium's analyzers; Quilter's impedance solver and DC IR beta; free: openEMS, padne, PyPEEC, FasterCap, ThermalSim, ngspice | PDN impedance curve at each power pin; IR-drop heatmap |
| **Rule check** | Geometry is inside a threshold someone chose | Every DRC and DFM tool; Quilter's eight PRCs; HyperLynx DRC; SIwave EMI Scanner | Decoupling cap within 1 cm of its pin; ground plane under a trace |
| **ML heuristic** | A learned policy produced this layout | Quilter's reinforcement-learning placer and router; Allegro X AI | "Physics-driven" auto-layout |

The earlier reports collapsed the second row into "unknown". The owner's page collapses rows two and three into "physics validation". Both lose information. A rule check that a decoupling capacitor sits within 1 cm is useful, but it is not a simulation of power-delivery impedance. A simulation is useful too, but it is not a proof.

## What each commercial capability actually does

### Quilter (checked; corrections applied)

Quilter's "physics validation" is **eight Physics Rule Checks (PRCs)**. Each one measures a geometric or analytic quantity and compares it with a tolerance ([PRC overview](https://docs.quilter.ai/physics-rule-checks-prcs/overview.md)). The tolerances on the doc pages are labelled as example acceptable ranges, not fixed defaults. The overview never mentions a field solver or a thermal simulation.

| PRC | What it measures | Applied to |
|---|---|---|
| Pin Distance | Euclidean distance between the closest pin edges, in cm (example range 0-1 cm) | Bypass caps, crystals, converters |
| Trace Path Length | Trace length between two pins, in cm (example 0-1 cm) | Same |
| Layer Switch Count | Vias on the path (example 0-1) | Same |
| Ground Plane Overlap | Boolean: passes when ground overlap of the trace is complete | Same, plus diff pairs and single-ended impedance nets |
| Invalid Width Span | % of length outside a tolerance on nominal width (±10% not confirmed: page not opened) | Single-ended impedance nets |
| Uncoupled Spacing | Length where pair spacing is outside a tolerance on nominal (page not opened) | Diff pairs |
| Length Mismatch | Length difference within a pair (page not opened) | Diff pairs |
| Overheated Length | % of length whose "approximate analytic temperature rise" exceeds 20 °C (example 0-10%). The width rule behind it is IPC-2221, an empirical curve fit, so "analytic" is Quilter's word, not a physics model | Power nets |

Timing-sensitive signals are listed as "Coming soon" with no PRC yet. The Overheated Length page contradicts itself: it defines overheating as a rise above 20 °C, but its pass criterion speaks of a rise "below a given threshold".

The physics enters upstream, when the geometry is chosen:

- **Impedance.** Since 2026-04-07 Quilter's calculated impedance profiles are "powered by Simbeor by Simberian", a commercial 2D field solver, and since 2026-06-30 it solves diff-pair geometry for any target impedance ([changelog](https://www.quilter.ai/changelog)). The single-ended impedance page still lists only 50 Ω and 75 Ω, at 5% tolerance. Quilter's stated scope is signals below 6 GHz, because its impedance calculations use quasi-static approximations that may be inaccurate above that ([what Quilter does well](https://docs.quilter.ai/about-quilter/what-quilter-does-well.md)).
- **Current.** Nets count as high-current when their names match Quilter's (unlisted) voltage or power patterns, or when they sit in a "Power" netclass. Widths come from "IPC calculations" on copper weight and layer thickness ([power nets](https://docs.quilter.ai/physics-constraints/power-nets.md)); only the March 2026 changelog names IPC-2221. Power nets default to 200 mA when the detected voltage is under 3 V and 500 mA when it is over 3 V, unless the user enters a current; neither page says what happens at exactly 3 V. The page says Quilter is "not (yet) designed for very high current" and recommends pours.
- **DC IR drop.** A **beta** point-to-point DC IR-drop simulation shipped on 2026-08-31, rendered on power nets with source and sink set during setup. The changelog says "It is not yet a full power-integrity sign-off". No changelog entry describes AC PDN impedance, plane resonance or decoupling-frequency analysis (absence in the changelog only).
- **Ranking.** The default "Recommended" sort orders candidates by fewest DRC violations, then routing completion, then passing Priority PRCs (power nets, diff pairs, single-ended impedance), then the most conservative fab rules, then fewest layers, then passing Other PRCs, then shortest traces ([sorting](https://docs.quilter.ai/candidate-review/sorting.md)). Three other sorts exist: Best PRCs, Easiest to Fab and Fewest Layers. Candidates with DRC errors are not surfaced for review at all ([candidate details](https://docs.quilter.ai/candidate-review/candidate-details.md)). Since 2026-09-30 the list shows Pass/Warn/Fail counts. The documentation never uses the word "scorecard".

What Quilter does **not** do: signal-integrity simulation (reflections, eye, crosstalk), AC power-delivery impedance, EMC or board-level thermal. Its own docs say "Quilter is an implementer, not a proofreader", that users remain responsible for "Signal simulation and validation", design reviews and DRCs ([what Quilter isn't](https://docs.quilter.ai/about-quilter/what-quilter-isnt.md)), and that PRCs help assess whether a candidate "is likely to perform as expected" ([reviewing PRCs](https://docs.quilter.ai/reviewing-prcs.md)). The FAQ says the opposite in marketing language: candidates are evaluated "through detailed physics simulations" and optimised for thermal, EMI and impedance. No documented PRC supports that line. (An earlier draft cited an EE Journal interview here; two searches could not find it, so it is dropped.)

**Real-hardware evidence** (vendor self-reported). Project Speedrun was an i.MX 8M Mini SOM plus baseboard: 843 parts, 5,141 pins, 8-layer HDI built by Sierra Circuits. Routing completion was 98%, from 27 h of Quilter runtime plus 38.5 h of human cleanup, against 428 h of quoted manual layout. It booted Linux with no respin, and LPDDR4 trained at full speed on first boot ([Speedrun](https://www.quilter.ai/project-speedrun)). Quilter's validation post lists as not yet done: PCIe link training and throughput, extended thermal and power characterisation, EMI/EMC scans, high-speed SI margining, long-duration reliability, and larger-batch unit variability. It says several units behaved alike but gives no unit count. At the fab, Sierra recalculated impedance for the stackup it could actually build: the SOM's 50 Ω single-ended outer-layer target became **62 Ω (+24%), approved at ±30%**, and the baseboard's 100 Ω differential target became 92 Ω, approved at ±20% ([DFM lessons](https://www.quilter.ai/blog/ai-designed-motherboard-dfm-dfa-lessons)). Quilter warns that impedance "may vary GREATLY" between the solver-determined width and what the fab calculates. These are the fab's calculations, not TDR or coupon measurements, so they show a gap between the designed stackup and the buildable one, not between a solver and hardware. Even that gap is about a quarter of the target.

**Limits** ([what Quilter does well](https://docs.quilter.ai/about-quilter/what-quilter-does-well.md)): 5,000 pins or fewer, 48 V or less, 10 A or less, signals under 6 GHz. The earlier draft also listed "through-vias only, no diff pairs on 2-layer boards, length matching not generally available"; that page does not state them, and no other source for them was checked, so they are **unverified**. It does no creepage checking, which matters for our mains relay board. Pricing is per routed pin and unpublished. The free tier is non-commercial and trains on uploads. No public API was found, so Circuit OS cannot call Quilter from a pipeline.

**Constraint detection** (read; not in the second pass). Quilter's "Circuit Comprehension" finds bypass caps, crystals, converters, power nets and diff pairs from naming and topology patterns (refdes X/Y for crystals, P/N suffixes for pairs). It misses crystals with series resistors, and it picks one converter capacitor "somewhat arbitrarily". This narrows the "intent-derived constraints: only us" claim in `PCB_STRATEGY.md`. Circuit OS still has the stronger source of constraints: its IR *knows* which capacitor decouples which pin, rather than guessing from names.

### The sign-off stacks (checked, except where noted)

These are the "best-in-class" physics tools. All are quote-only and priced in the tens of thousands of dollars.

- **Siemens HyperLynx PI** extracts the whole power-delivery network and computes impedance at each power pin, pass/fail against a target, with a decoupling optimiser. **HyperLynx DRC** is a separate rule checker with 90+ rules across SI, PI, EMI and safety, scriptable in Python. Siemens itself calls DRC the "first stage" of verification, with simulation after it.
- **Cadence Sigrity PowerSI** is a 2.5D solver for S-parameters, PDN impedance, cavity resonance and near- and far-field radiation. **OptimizePI** chooses decaps on the same engine. **PowerDC** does DC IR drop with electrical-thermal co-simulation. **Celsius** does FEA plus CFD thermal. **Clarity** is a 3D full-wave FEM solver.
- **Ansys SIwave** does full-wave PDN extraction, an impedance and crosstalk scan, automated decaps, and DC loss into Icepak thermal. It can run headless through PyEDB (MIT), but only with a licensed AEDT. Its **EMI Scanner** is 17 geometric rules with no dB result.
- **Keysight PIPro** covers DC IR drop, DC electro-thermal, AC PDN impedance and plane resonance with Q factors, plus a decap BOM optimiser. **SIPro** handles the SI side.

No vendor claims proof. The strongest stated guarantee is vendor-published correlation to lab measurement on specific fixtures (Clarity to 50 GHz on one IEEE P370 fixture). We found no published percent-error figure for DC IR drop or PDN impedance against measurement. **EMC in every tool is a rule scan, plus an optional full-wave solve that an engineer compares with limits by hand.** One board-level study put simulated far-field E within about 10 dB of chamber measurement (summary).

### AI-PCB competitors (Flux impedance, JITX and Cadstrom checked; the rest read)

- **Flux** has a beta **ngspice simulator** (transient, AC, DC, operating point), with prompt-driven simulation since March 2026 ([docs](https://docs.flux.ai/reference/simulator-tool)). It supports up to **8 layers** and has linear auto-versioning with restore (read). Its impedance routing is checked: Flux generates Keep In, Keep Out and Trace Width rules from the part's impedance properties and the selected stackup, and after routing shows the parameters used and a net-length property ([advanced routing](https://docs.flux.ai/tutorials/advanced-routing)). That is a pre-route width and spacing rule. The pages checked document no post-route impedance verification and no SI or PI simulation.
- **JITX** is the only AI-PCB tool found with a full 3D solver in the loop. It "sets up ports, sweeps, boundaries, and metrics from requirements", dispatches Ansys HFSS runs and feeds the results into the next code edit. Its homepage example optimises a PCIe gen-7 BGA launch and deskew at 56 GHz Nyquist to -30 dB return loss and -40 dB mode conversion in five HFSS simulations ([jitx.com](https://jitx.com/)). That is a vendor example, not a benchmark; the physics is HFSS's.
- **Cadstrom** lists checks of "voltage compatibility between interfacing components", logic polarity, "component ratings and margins" and pin and signal assignments, by "hybrid analysis (AI + deterministic checks)" with rules and simulation behind each finding ([cadstrom.io](https://www.cadstrom.io/)). No public technical documentation says how the rules or simulation work, so treat it as AI plus rules, backed by marketing. **Circuit Mind** generates stress, derating, power and FMECA reports (read). Both weaken "only you" claims on the validation rows.
- **Celus, DeepPCB, Diode and SnapMagic** do no post-layout physics. DeepPCB explicitly leaves impedance and length matching to the user.

### DFM reports and voltage/current checks (checked, except Valor, PCBflow and Eurocircuits)

Every DFM tool examined is a **geometric rule check against a capability table**. That covers Altium's manufacturing rules, Valor NPI and PCBflow (1,000+ checks), JLCPCB's DFM, NextPCB HQ DFM, Aisler and Eurocircuits. None simulates physics. Category names mislead: HQ DFM's "Signal Integrity" category is, in its own plugin source, five checks: missing trace (spelt "Trace Mssing"), acute-angle traces, dangling tracks, floating copper and unconnected vias ([rule_catalog.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Huaqiu-Electronics/kicad-hqdfm-plugin/HEAD/kicad_dfm/core/rule_catalog.py)). The implementation tests connectivity and junction angles; nothing models impedance, crosstalk or reflection. NextPCB's own check counts do not agree: "1200+" on its download page, while its DFM manual reportedly says about 150 (search result only). JLCDFM is a web GUI with five analysis modules, a "30+ point checklist" and a PDF report.

Our reports said "no fab offers a CI/API DFM check". That is **out of date as written**. NextPCB/Huaqiu's open-source KiCad plugin uploads a Gerber zip over HTTP to `eda.cn/openapi` and polls for a JSON response that holds a URL to the result file; the code does not say what format that file is ([remote.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Huaqiu-Electronics/kicad-hqdfm-plugin/HEAD/kicad_dfm/services/remote.py)). The overseas route polls a separate `DfmView/getParseResult` path; the domestic route depends on a hardcoded session cookie. It is scriptable, but it is an undocumented private API. Aisler has offered "Push for CLI" since 2026-02-06: an S3 upload that creates a project in your workspace, with no check results returned ([Aisler](https://community.aisler.net/t/quick-and-secure-import-using-aisler-push-for-cli/5527)). A trade-press item dated 2026-06-17 reports that Boardera launched a Decode API returning structured data that includes DFM results, with "version-controlled JSON schema support" and "integrated SpeedDFM analysis" listed as separate features ([Circuits Assembly](https://circuitsassembly.com/ca/editorial/menu-new-indust-prod/43365-boardera-launches-decode-api-for-pcb-manufacturing-automation.html)); Boardera's own API docs were not read. Valor NPI can be scripted in Python (read). JLCPCB's developer portal lists PCB, stencil, 3D-printing and component APIs and publicly lists no DFM module ([api.jlcpcb.com](https://api.jlcpcb.com)). None of these is yet a documented, licensed DFM API we could build a release gate on. The practical conclusion stands, but the sentence must change.

For voltage-aware schematic checking, the real commercial product is **Siemens Xpedition Schematic Analysis** (from Valydate, which joined Mentor in August 2017). It "leverages over 150 voltage-aware rules" ([EEJournal, Nov 2018](https://www.eejournal.com/article/validate-twice-build-once/); the count is vendor-stated and may be out of date), builds models from the manufacturer part numbers in the BOM, checks every net in single- and multi-board systems for mismatched voltage levels, pin types and connections, and flags capacitor derating and missing pull-ups ([Siemens, 2020](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/electronic-systems-design/2020/06/22/how-can-i-catch-schematic-errors-more-easily/); Siemens, 2023). Cadence's nearest equivalent is PSpice "Smoke" analysis, which is simulation rather than a rule check: it compares simulated stresses with safe limits computed from the manufacturer's maximum operating conditions and user derating factors, with power derated by junction temperature for semiconductors ([EMA](https://www.ema-eda.com/how-to-page/how-to-analyze-component-stress-with-pspice/)). It is only as good as its models and stimulus. Whether Cadence's own schematic checks (System Capture) have any voltage-aware rule is **not established**; the page cited for it covers only MTBF estimation. Altium's ERC has none of this.

## What Circuit OS can build

The ranking puts the cheapest physics-grade result first. Each item names the rung it earns. **A new rung is needed**, between *bounded* and *evidence*:

> **simulated**: a physics solver's result on a named model with a named tool and version. Its model error is not validated against hardware until a coupon measurement calibrates it.

And *unknown* splits into **not checkable**, **checkable by simulation, not run (reason)** and **checkable, tool named**. This labelling change alone answers most of the owner's objection, and it takes hours.

## What nobody can claim, with every tool bought

- **That a board works.** Every result is about a model. For Quilter's own 50 Ω target, the fab's own stackup calculation gave 62 Ω.
- **EMC compliance.** Commercial EMC is rule scans plus solves an engineer compares by hand, and board-level emission prediction carries order-10 dB uncertainty. Only a chamber settles it.
- **Accuracy of these free tools against measurement.** None of gerber2ems, padne, ThermalSim, kicad-parasitics or EMF Inspector publishes a quantitative error on FR-4 in the sources checked. Antmicro compared its gerber2ems/openEMS flow with VNA measurements on a DDR5 RDIMM tester, but only qualitatively ("match pretty well"), with no error figure. padne's NLnet grant lists measurement validation as planned work. EMF Inspector states that its absolute fields carry one to two orders of magnitude of uncertainty against full-wave simulation. Their outputs are physics or heuristic estimates with unquantified model error, until our coupons calibrate them. Not every free tool lacks numbers: the KiCad Fill Resistance plugin reports DC values within 20% of a micro-ohm meter.
- **Pin-map correctness from physics.** A wrong pinout passes every simulator. Only an independent pin source or a powered test catches it. This is still the most likely way for one of our boards to fail.
